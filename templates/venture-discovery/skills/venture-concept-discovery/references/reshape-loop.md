# The Reshape-the-Wedge Loop

The most valuable move in concept discovery. A low rubric is rarely a verdict — it's usually pointing at the wrong wedge, not a dead idea. Before you write NO-GO, try to reshape the concept into the version the evidence supports, then re-run the sections the reshape changes.

## Why reshape instead of reject
The evidence that kills a pitch usually kills a *specific configuration* of it — a payer, a channel, a mechanic — not the underlying pain. The pain is often real; the packaging is wrong. Reshaping asks: given everything that's falsified, what is the smallest configuration that survives? In one reference pass (a family-caregiving concept), the consumer neobank was dead on arrival (dead comps, no willingness-to-pay, unbuildable mechanic), but the same underlying pain supported a defensible read-only B2B coordination tool sold to professionals who already bill for the work. Same pain, different everything else.

## The standard reframes (run each against the failing sections)

**1. Payer ≠ sufferer → bill the party with money and authority.**
If the sufferer can't or won't pay, who has the money and the standing to authorize? Often the care-recipient's estate, an employer, an insurer, or a professional fiduciary. Re-ask sizing and willingness-to-pay against *that* payer.

**2. D2C → B2B2C through whoever already touches the trigger.**
If you can't reach the buyer at the acute moment via paid acquisition, who already does — discharge planners, advisors, attorneys, HR, the bank? Selling through the channel that owns the trigger event often converts where direct never could. Re-ask GTM and distribution.

**3. Linear-labor → agentic force-multiplier.**
If the category has been a graveyard because the service is expensive linear human labor (and thus a vitamin nobody pays for at scale), ask whether agents change the margin and the pain math — selling a caseload-multiplier to a professional who already bills the labor turns a vitamin into ROI. This reframe attacks *two* structural killers at once (cost structure + willingness-to-pay). Re-ask business model, buildability, and the buyer.

**4. Roll-up → single-wedge depth.**
If the pitch folds many features together ("OS for X"), the Ramp lesson is depth within one buyer before breadth across buyers. Reshape to the one wedge that earns the right to the next, and name what's deferred.

## Adjacency / analog mapping
When a wedge works, map where the *same engine* applies with a different trigger event — adjacent verticals reachable without rebuilding the core (e.g. a document-and-portal engine built for one fiduciary use case reaching estate/trust admin, guardianship, medical-billing advocacy). Score adjacencies on a consistent filter (is the buyer billable, is the pain concentrated, is the domain underserved, how catastrophic is an early error) and keep them *reachable but unbuilt* in year one. This is how you preserve optionality without falling back into the roll-up trap.

## When to stop reshaping and call NO-GO
Reshaping is not infinite optimism. Call NO-GO when *every* reframe collides with the **same** structural wall:
- an **unbuildable mechanic** the concept can't work without (and won't exist on near-term rails),
- **uninsurable / unbounded liability** no configuration removes,
- a **category revenue ceiling** so low that even the best-shaped version isn't worth the effort for the people involved (note: this may still be a fine bootstrapped business — say so rather than a flat NO).

If two or three independent reframes all die on the same wall, that wall is the real finding. Write it plainly.
