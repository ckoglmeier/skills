# Verdict, Rubric & Discovery Plan — Output Structure

The final synthesis leg produces exactly these five parts, in order. Keep it skimmable; a reader should get the verdict in the first paragraph and the "what to do next" without hunting.

## 1. Executive verdict
One paragraph. Open with the call in caps — **GO** / **RESHAPE-THE-WEDGE** / **NO-GO** — then the reasoning in evidence, not vibes: the two or three facts that drove it. End with **the one sentence to remember** — the single line that captures the whole finding (e.g. "The pain is real but the payer isn't — every consumer version is already dead, so the only version worth building is the narrow B2B wedge that earns institutional distribution").

If RESHAPE, the verdict must say *what it reshapes into* — the surviving version — not just "the pitch is wrong."

## 2. Scored rubric
Score each dimension 1–5 with a one-line, evidence-cited justification. Let scores move only where the evidence actually moves them, and call out the load-bearing lows explicitly.

| Dimension | What a low score means |
|---|---|
| **Market size (bottoms-up SOM)** | Convertible dollars are small even if the population is large; comps cap out low |
| **Pain urgency** | Chronic-and-tolerated (vitamin), or the acute trigger is un-targetable |
| **Willingness to pay** | Revealed preference says the payer won't pay at any tested price (dead/free comps) |
| **Regulatory risk** (lower score = higher risk) | Stacks multiple regimes; hard to ship; uninsurable |
| **Technical complexity** (lower score = more complex) | Headline mechanic doesn't exist on current rails; long build |
| **Distribution advantage** | Every proven channel is closed/occupied; only D2C-into-crisis remains |
| **Timing** | Window is closing (consolidation) or hasn't opened (rails/tailwinds absent) |

Name the one or two dimensions that should stop the concept cold if they're 1s — usually willingness-to-pay and distribution.

## 3. Committed wedge / beachhead
The single feature that ships first, to the single ICP, monetized one way — and an explicit list of what is **NOT** built in year one despite being in the brief. This is where you resist the roll-up temptation. One line: `WEDGE/BEACHHEAD = [x], because […]`.

## 4. Pre-committed kill gates
Falsifiable thresholds, committed *before* building, that must clear or the concept is killed. Good gates are specific and binary: "≥N buyer interviews confirm they'll pay $X," "reliability bar met on a real (not demo) test set," "one signed design partner by month M," "insurability confirmed in writing." Pre-committing them is what stops a discovery pass from becoming a rationalization exercise.

## 5. Concrete near-term discovery plan (typically 90 days)
- **Interview segments** — a table of segment, target N, and the *specific* recruiting channel (named directory, subreddit, association, conference — not "reach out to some users"). Include deliberate *exclusions* with reasons.
- **The riskiest "go" assumptions** — the 3–5 assumptions the whole thesis rests on, each with a **falsifiable test** and a threshold. If an assumption can't be phrased as something that could come back false, it's not a test.
- **A no-build rule** — what is explicitly not built during discovery (usually: no product code; a manual/concierge pilot is the only permitted "build"), and a day-N go/no-go memo rule committed on day 1.

## Style
Blunt over hedged. If willingness-to-pay is a 1, say "nobody has ever gotten this buyer to pay at scale" and cite the corpses. A discovery report earns its keep by being the document that says the hard thing before the money is spent.
